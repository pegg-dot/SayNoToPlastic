HOW SCIENTISTS DETECT
MICROPLASTICS
Finding the Invisible
Microplastics and nanoplastics are far too small to be seen with the naked eye. Some are
thinner than a human hair, while the smallest nanoplastics are measured in billionths of a meter.

So how do scientists know they are present in our bodies?

The answer lies in advanced laboratory technology capable of identifying not only the size of
these particles but also the type of plastic they are made from.

As research has advanced, these techniques have allowed scientists to detect microplastics in
blood, the brain, arteries, lungs, reproductive organs, the placenta, breast milk, and many other
human tissues.




Why Detection Matters
Before scientists can understand whether microplastics affect human health, they must first
answer a simpler question:

Are they actually there?

Over the past decade, improvements in laboratory technology have made it possible to detect
microscopic plastic particles that previously went unnoticed.

This has transformed the field of environmental health, allowing researchers to move beyond
speculation and begin measuring human exposure directly.




How Scientists Find Microplastics
Detecting microplastics begins with collecting a sample, such as blood, tissue, breast milk, or
placental tissue.
The sample is carefully prepared to remove fats, proteins, and other biological material while
preserving any plastic particles that may be present.

Researchers then analyze the remaining material using highly specialized instruments capable
of identifying different types of plastic.

These technologies can determine not only whether plastic particles are present, but also their
size, shape, and chemical composition.

Commonly identified plastics include polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), polyethylene
terephthalate (PET), polystyrene (PS), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC)—materials commonly found
in food packaging, bottles, clothing, household products, and countless everyday items.




Can We Trust These Studies?
This is one of the most important questions in environmental health.

Because plastic is everywhere, researchers must take extraordinary precautions to avoid
contaminating their samples during collection and analysis.

Modern studies use strict laboratory protocols, specialized equipment, and multiple
quality-control measures to minimize contamination.

As technology continues to improve, scientists are becoming increasingly confident in their
ability to accurately identify and measure microplastics in human tissues.

Like all areas of science, however, methods continue to evolve, and researchers are constantly
refining their techniques to improve accuracy and consistency.




What This Means
Finding microplastics in the human body is no longer the question.

Multiple research groups around the world have independently confirmed their presence in a
growing number of human tissues.

The next challenge is understanding what these findings mean for human health.

As laboratory methods continue to improve, researchers will be better equipped to answer
questions about how these particles enter the body, where they accumulate, how long they
remain, and whether they contribute to disease.
The science is moving quickly, and each new discovery brings us one step closer to
understanding the full picture.
