THE DIGESTIVE SYSTEM
The Gateway Between the Outside World and Your Body
Every day, your digestive system processes everything you eat and drink, extracting the
nutrients your body needs while helping eliminate waste. It is also one of the primary ways we
are exposed to microplastics and nanoplastics.

Food, drinking water, and even the air we swallow while eating can introduce microscopic
plastic particles into the digestive tract. As a result, the gastrointestinal system has become one
of the most important areas of research in understanding how these particles interact with the
human body.




Why the Digestive System Matters
The digestive system is much more than a tube that processes food.

It contains nearly 70% of the body’s immune cells, houses trillions of beneficial bacteria
known as the gut microbiome, and forms a protective barrier that carefully regulates what enters
the bloodstream.

When this barrier functions properly, it allows nutrients to pass through while keeping many
harmful substances out. Researchers are now studying whether long-term exposure to
microplastics may affect this delicate balance.




What the Research Has Found
Scientists have detected microplastics throughout the digestive tract, including in human stool
samples, confirming that these particles are routinely consumed and pass through the
gastrointestinal system.

Laboratory studies suggest that some very small particles may cross the intestinal barrier and
enter the bloodstream, while the majority appear to pass through the body and are eliminated.

Researchers are also investigating whether microplastics may influence the gut microbiome,
increase intestinal inflammation, or alter the integrity of the intestinal barrier. Most of this
evidence currently comes from laboratory and animal studies, and its significance for human
health is still being determined.

At present, there is no definitive evidence that dietary microplastics directly cause digestive
diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease, Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, or colon
cancer. However, the digestive system remains one of the most active areas of ongoing
research.




What This Means
Every meal represents an interaction between your body and the outside world.

The discovery that microplastics routinely enter the digestive system is not surprising given how
widespread plastic has become in modern life. The more important question is whether
repeated exposure over decades has meaningful biological effects.

Scientists are working to understand how much of what we consume remains within the
digestive tract, how much enters the circulation, and whether long-term exposure influences
inflammation, the gut microbiome, or overall health.

While many questions remain unanswered, these findings reinforce the importance of studying
the relationship between our environment and the foods we eat.
